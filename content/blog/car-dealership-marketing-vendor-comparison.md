<p>A car dealership marketing vendor comparison should look beyond the number of leads in a proposal. Dealers need to understand how a vendor will attract relevant shoppers, what happens when someone responds, how results will be measured, and which parts depend on the store. Comparing the work behind a forecast gives you a more useful basis for choosing a partner than comparing promised lead volume alone.</p><p data-mega-cta="primary"><a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/contact">Talk with our team about your dealership</a></p><h2>What should a dealership compare besides lead volume?</h2><p>Lead volume is easy to place on a slide. It does not explain whether the campaign fits your store, whether inquiries can be handled, or whether the dealership can learn from the results. A practical comparison covers the entire route from campaign exposure to shopper conversation and next step.</p><p>Assess each vendor across business fit, audience and offer, creative, inquiry handling, measurement, reporting, and communication. A provider may be strong in one area and weak in another. For example, an appealing campaign may generate form submissions, but its business value is difficult to assess if no one owns prompt follow-up or the store cannot tell whether submissions became appointments.</p><p>Before requesting proposals, write down the dealership’s priorities. Include the vehicles or inventory themes to support, the shoppers you want to reach, the sales team’s capacity to respond, and the outcomes you want to understand. Use the same starting information and questions for each candidate. Review BDC Promotions’ <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/services">automotive marketing services</a> as one example of how campaign activity and shopper engagement can be considered together.</p><p>That preparation matters because the best vendor is not necessarily the one with the biggest projection. It is the one whose proposed work aligns with your objective, can be operated by the people involved, and can be evaluated with information the dealership can actually collect.</p><h2>How can you test whether a vendor understands your store?</h2><p>A proposal should reflect the dealership’s actual goals and operating conditions. Ask the vendor to explain what it learned about your store and how that information shaped its recommendation. A plan that could be sent unchanged to any dealership may not account for your inventory priorities, staffing, or customer journey.</p><ul><li><strong>Inventory and focus:</strong> Which vehicle types, models, or shopper needs will the campaign feature? How will priorities be revisited when inventory or availability changes?</li><li><strong>Audience:</strong> Who is the campaign intended to reach? What makes the message relevant to those shoppers rather than a broad, generic audience?</li><li><strong>Offer and next step:</strong> What action should a shopper take, and what will the shopper see or receive after taking it?</li><li><strong>Store capacity:</strong> Who handles new inquiries, and what response process can the dealership sustain during busy periods and staff absences?</li><li><strong>Constraints:</strong> Which outcomes depend on factors the vendor cannot control, such as inventory updates, store hours, approvals, or the dealership’s response availability?</li></ul><p>Listen for a clear explanation of assumptions and open questions, not a guarantee. A capable partner should identify information it still needs, explain what it can influence, and describe how it would respond when priorities change. Ask for a concrete example: if a featured model becomes unavailable, who updates the campaign, how quickly can changes be reviewed, and what is the approval path?</p><p>It can also help to distinguish the store’s problem from the requested tactic. If the objective is more useful sales conversations, ask how the vendor expects its proposed channel and message to contribute. Do not accept a channel recommendation simply because it is familiar or easy to put into a package. The vendor should connect the proposed activity to a real shopper action and a dealership process.</p><h2>What should you ask about campaign strategy and creative?</h2><p>Ask each candidate to walk through one sample campaign from the first idea to the shopper’s next step. The explanation should connect audience, message, creative, placement, and destination. If these pieces do not fit together, the campaign may get attention without giving a shopper a useful reason to respond.</p><p>Request examples that are relevant to your dealership, then ask what makes them relevant. Clarify who supplies photos or video, who writes the message, how review works, and what can be changed after feedback. Confirm how the vendor will prevent outdated inventory details, expired offers, or inconsistent store information from reaching shoppers.</p><p>Judge creative on clarity and relevance, not polish alone. A strong opening, a specific reason to pay attention, and an obvious next action are more useful than attractive but unrelated footage. Ask whether the vendor tests alternate messages or formats, what evidence leads it to keep or revise an approach, and how it separates a creative issue from an audience or follow-up issue. This guide to <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/automotive-dealership-ad-creative-testing">automotive dealership ad creative testing</a> offers another set of questions to use when reviewing the work.</p><p>Advertising claims and offer details deserve a defined review, too. For example, a federal dealer advertising guide explains that a lease ad using a payment as a trigger term can require additional disclosures. Ask who checks applicable offer details and disclosures before launch, and confirm the rules that apply to your specific campaign with qualified counsel or a compliance professional. See the <a href="https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2018/WorkGroups/House%20Commerce/Bills/S.136/S.136~Clare%20Buckley~A%20Dealer%20Guide%20to%20Federal%20Advertising%20Requirements~4-12-2017.pdf" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">dealer guide to federal advertising requirements</a>; it is a reference, not legal advice or a substitute for checking current requirements.</p><p>Finally, ask what happens between approval and launch. Who confirms the final version, where will the approved files be kept, and how does the dealership know that the campaign being launched is the one it reviewed? A documented approval checkpoint reduces confusion and helps avoid rushed changes to sensitive offer details.</p><h2>How will shopper inquiries be handled?</h2><p>Marketing does not end when a shopper submits a form or sends a message. Before selecting a vendor, map the handoff. Establish which team receives each inquiry, where it appears, who responds, and how the next action is recorded. The marketing partner and dealership should agree on the boundary between campaign work and store follow-up.</p><ol><li>Confirm the inquiry source and destination. Ask to see the shopper’s experience and where the dealership receives the information.</li><li>Name an owner for incoming inquiries, including coverage when the usual person is unavailable.</li><li>Set a response expectation the store can meet, then decide how delayed or missed responses will be noticed.</li><li>Agree on useful status labels, such as reached, appointment requested, appointment set, or unable to contact.</li><li>Review a sample inquiry together and confirm that the shopper receives an appropriate next step.</li></ol><p>Ask whether the vendor’s role includes responding to leads or whether the dealership owns that work. Do not assume that lead generation includes appointment setting or ongoing shopper communication. Put the division of responsibility in writing, including who troubleshoots a failed delivery or an inquiry that arrives without enough information.</p><p>For a practical view of a campaign-to-follow-up sequence, compare the vendor’s proposal with BDC Promotions’ <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/process">campaign and lead-handling process</a>. You can also review this guide to <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/dealership-lead-follow-up-scripts">dealership lead follow-up scripts</a> when checking whether the store has a consistent approach for the conversations its marketing creates.</p><p>Consider a simple example. A campaign produces several inquiries while the sales team is short-staffed. If the proposal only defines how inquiries are generated, there may be no agreed owner for the resulting conversations. If the plan names a dealership contact, a backup, a delivery location, and a review routine, the team can spot the bottleneck and decide what to change. The lead count alone cannot reveal that operational difference.</p><h2>Which results should vendors report?</h2><p>Ask for a measurement plan before launch, not after the first report arrives. It should define each metric, where the data comes from, how often it will be reviewed, and who is responsible for keeping the records usable. The goal is not an impressive dashboard. It is consistent information that helps the dealership decide what to do next.</p><p>Use a sequence of measures rather than relying on a single lead count:</p><ul><li><strong>Campaign activity:</strong> Spend, reach, impressions, and engagement, where available.</li><li><strong>Inquiries:</strong> Submissions or conversations attributed to the campaign, with duplicate or unusable records identified consistently.</li><li><strong>Contact and progress:</strong> Whether the dealership reached the shopper and whether the conversation advanced.</li><li><strong>Appointments:</strong> Appointments set and, if the dealership tracks it, appointments that arrived.</li><li><strong>Business outcomes:</strong> Sales or other agreed outcomes, with attribution limits explained rather than hidden.</li></ul><p>Define what an actionable inquiry means for your store. You might require valid contact details and a relevant vehicle interest, while recording other cases separately. Agree how the team will classify duplicates, test records, shoppers seeking something unavailable, and inquiries that cannot be reached. A shared definition makes the discussion more productive than arguing over whether all submissions are equally valuable.</p><p>The vendor should explain what it can measure directly and what depends on timely, accurate dealership records. If you need a starting structure for discussing qualification, see the <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/dealership-lead-quality-scoring">dealership lead quality scoring framework</a>. Keep the scoring rules practical enough that staff can use them consistently rather than creating labels no one has time to maintain.</p><p>Attribution has limits. A shopper may see an ad, return later through another route, or contact the dealership in a way that is not cleanly connected to the first campaign. Ask how the vendor assigns credit, what the report may undercount or overcount, and whether different reports use different attribution windows. A transparent limitation is more useful than a precise-looking number without context.</p><h2>How do vendors differ in reporting and accountability?</h2><p>Reports should answer practical questions: What happened? What changed? What does the vendor recommend next? A recurring list of totals is not a useful review if nobody explains how those totals relate to the dealership’s goal.</p><p>Ask to see a sample report with sensitive information removed. Check whether it separates campaign activity from dealership outcomes, identifies the dates being compared, and explains notable changes. Confirm how often you will review performance, how the vendor will flag a material issue, and whom you contact when a question needs attention.</p><p>Accountability includes candor about uncertainty and action. Ask for an example of how the vendor responds when results miss expectations. A useful answer explains what it would inspect first: audience, offer, creative, inquiry routing, and the dealership’s response process. It should say which changes the vendor can make and which require your approval.</p><p>Make communication expectations concrete. Record a primary contact, a backup route for urgent questions, the regular update cadence, and how decisions are documented. Ask for a realistic process and timeline for new creative or a material revision. This is not administrative detail: without it, a useful recommendation can stall while both sides assume the other person owns the next step.</p><h2>What should a vendor comparison scorecard include?</h2><p>Use the same scorecard for each candidate. Rate every area from 1 to 5: 1 means the answer was unclear or unsupported; 3 means it was plausible but left important details open; and 5 means it was specific, relevant, and supported by a workable process. Add evidence and unresolved questions beside each score. A rating without notes can hide the difference between a polished presentation and an executable plan.</p><table><thead><tr><th>Evaluation area</th><th>What to look for</th><th>Question to ask</th><th>Score</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Business fit</td><td>Connection to store goals, inventory priorities, and team capacity</td><td>How does this plan reflect our current priorities?</td><td>1–5</td></tr><tr><td>Campaign approach</td><td>Audience, message, creative, and destination work as a coherent plan</td><td>Can you explain one campaign through to shopper action?</td><td>1–5</td></tr><tr><td>Creative and approval</td><td>Relevant assets, clear review steps, and controlled launch approval</td><td>Who reviews and approves each final asset?</td><td>1–5</td></tr><tr><td>Lead handoff</td><td>Named owners and a practical inquiry response path</td><td>Who receives each inquiry, and what falls outside your scope?</td><td>1–5</td></tr><tr><td>Measurement</td><td>Defined metrics, data sources, attribution limits, and review cadence</td><td>How will we distinguish inquiries from appointments and sales?</td><td>1–5</td></tr><tr><td>Communication</td><td>Reachable contacts, clear approvals, and timely issue escalation</td><td>Who updates us and when will you flag a problem?</td><td>1–5</td></tr><tr><td>Adaptability</td><td>Evidence-based learning and a defined process for changes</td><td>What would you investigate if the campaign misses its goal?</td><td>1–5</td></tr></tbody></table><p>Set weights based on the dealership’s real need. A store that already has steady inquiry volume but inconsistent follow-up may give greater weight to handoff and measurement. A dealership launching a new campaign may emphasize strategy, creative, and approval. Keep the weights consistent when comparing vendors, then discuss any unusually low or high score before deciding.</p><p>Make the notes specific. Instead of writing “good reporting,” record that the vendor demonstrated a sample report with inquiry source, appointment status, date range, and attribution caveats. Instead of writing “unclear follow-up,” note exactly which role or handoff is missing. This creates a record that another decision-maker can review without relying on memory of the sales presentation.</p><h2>How should you compare proposals and costs?</h2><p>Do not compare totals until you know what each proposal includes. Separate service fees from advertising spend and identify any other costs. Confirm what the dealership must supply, whether there are setup tasks, what creative and reporting work is included, and what requires a separate agreement.</p><p>Then compare proposed work with the dealership’s goals and staffing. A lower quote may leave important creative, data, or follow-up tasks to the store. A higher quote may include work the dealership does not need. The more useful comparison is the cost and effort required to operate the full plan and assess its contribution, not simply the price per submitted lead.</p><p>BDC Promotions publicly describes dealership campaigns as starting at $2,500 and recommends ad spend of $100 per day; the published page does not specify a billing interval for the starting price. Treat those figures as public guidance, not as a complete custom proposal, and confirm current scope and terms directly. See the <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/pricing">dealership consultation and service options</a> page for context. When reviewing any vendor’s quote, ask for an itemized description of the work and a clear distinction between service fees and media spend.</p><p>For a fair comparison, put each proposal into the same categories: strategy and setup, creative production, media management, response or engagement work, reporting, and any store-side labor. Note what is included, what is excluded, and what assumptions could change the total. If the proposals use different time periods or different definitions of a lead, normalize those before comparing them.</p><h2>What questions should you ask before choosing?</h2><p>Use the same questions in a final conversation with each vendor. Ask for specifics and note whether the answers match what was written in the proposal.</p><ul><li>What information do you need from our store before recommending a campaign?</li><li>Which outcomes are you responsible for, and which depend on our team?</li><li>How does a shopper move from an ad or message to a dealership conversation?</li><li>What will the report show, and what cannot be attributed reliably?</li><li>How do creative review, approval, and campaign changes work?</li><li>Who is our day-to-day contact, and what is the route for an urgent question?</li><li>What would cause you to recommend a change, and how would you explain the decision?</li><li>What should we prepare internally so the campaign and follow-up can work as intended?</li><li>What is the expected timeline for setup, review, launch, and the first performance discussion?</li><li>What records or access will the dealership need to provide, and how will responsibilities be documented?</li></ul><p>Look for direct, operational answers. If a response is vague, request a concrete example or a written description. The discussion itself can show whether the vendor listens, clarifies assumptions, and respects the dealership’s decision process. Compare what each person says with the proposal rather than treating a reassuring conversation as a substitute for clear scope.</p><h2>What warning signs deserve a closer look?</h2><p>A high lead forecast is not automatically a problem, but ask what assumptions support it and what happens if actual results differ. Pause when a proposal skips the inquiry handoff, treats every submission as equally useful, or promises outcomes without explaining dependencies.</p><ul><li><strong>Unclear scope:</strong> The proposal does not identify who creates assets, monitors campaigns, handles inquiries, or reports results.</li><li><strong>One-size-fits-all plan:</strong> The vendor cannot explain how the strategy changes with your goals or operating conditions.</li><li><strong>Vanity metrics alone:</strong> The report emphasizes reach or clicks but does not connect them to inquiries and dealership follow-up.</li><li><strong>No approval clarity:</strong> Nobody can explain who reviews creative or authorizes launch and material changes.</li><li><strong>Unreachable support:</strong> The vendor cannot identify a contact or a reasonable route for important questions.</li><li><strong>Unexplained guarantees:</strong> A promised result has no stated assumptions, exclusions, or measurement method.</li><li><strong>Unowned data gaps:</strong> The vendor expects the dealership to provide records but cannot explain which records or who will check them.</li></ul><p>These are reasons to ask for clarification, not proof that a vendor cannot deliver. Give each candidate the same opportunity to explain the plan in writing. If the answer remains unclear, record the uncertainty in your scorecard instead of filling the gap with assumptions.</p><h2>How can a dealership make the final decision?</h2><p>First, identify the two or three outcomes that matter most to your store and the internal conditions needed to support them. Next, compare the proposals against the same scorecard and record evidence for each rating. Confirm that scope, reporting, approvals, and inquiry responsibilities are written down. Choose the plan your team can operate and evaluate, not simply the plan with the most attractive forecast.</p><p>Before signing, read the proposal and the final scope together. Check that the selected services, fees, media budget, responsibilities, reporting measures, and cancellation or change terms are clear. If the proposal uses a term such as qualified lead, ask for its written definition. Clarify how the vendor will handle a campaign pause, an inventory change, or a delay in approving new creative.</p><p>Set a review date and agree in advance on what information will be available then. Early performance may reflect setup, campaign learning, inventory changes, or the dealership’s response process. Do not judge from one isolated number. Review the agreed measures, identify bottlenecks, and document each adjustment and its reason. If results are mixed, separate what the vendor controls from what the store controls before deciding what to change.</p><p data-mega-cta="secondary"><a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/services">Explore dealership marketing services</a></p><h2>Frequently asked questions</h2><h3>Should I choose the vendor that promises the most leads?</h3><p>Not on that number alone. Ask how the estimate was formed, what the vendor counts as a lead, how inquiries will be handled, and how the dealership will judge quality and progress. Compare the assumptions and operating plan behind each forecast.</p><h3>What is the most important metric to compare?</h3><p>There is no single metric that fits every dealership. Start with the business outcome you want to understand, then examine the measures that connect campaign activity to inquiries, contact, appointments, and any agreed sales outcome. Make sure each measure has a clear definition and data source.</p><h3>How many vendors should a dealership compare?</h3><p>Compare enough candidates to understand the available approaches, but use the same brief and scorecard for each one. A consistent process is more useful than collecting many proposals built on different assumptions.</p><h3>What should be in writing before a campaign launches?</h3><p>Document the scope, fees and media spend, campaign objective, approval process, inquiry handoff, reporting definitions, contacts, and change procedure. Confirm that final creative and offer details have been approved by the appropriate dealership decision-maker.</p><p>A careful comparison turns a vendor decision into a review of the whole operating plan, from the first message to the dealership’s next conversation with a shopper.
