<p>An automotive dealership digital marketing audit is a structured review of how shoppers find the store, respond to an offer, and reach a scheduled next step. It is not a media plan and it is not a score of which channel is "best." It asks whether the current path still matches the vehicles, offers, and follow-up the store can actually support. A useful audit leaves a short list of checks the general manager, marketing lead, and business development team can walk together.</p><p data-mega-cta="primary"><strong><a href="tel:+13522071074">Call (352) 207-1074 to review your dealership's audit</a></strong></p><p>BDC Promotions works with franchise and established independent dealerships on the path from advertising to a shopper conversation. The public site describes five connected steps: strategy, creative, optimization, nurture, and a scheduled appointment. Dealerships can choose one piece or connect the lane. This checklist follows that same path. It does not promise a lead count, a cost per lead, or a sales result.</p><h2>What an automotive dealership digital marketing audit should decide</h2><p>Start with a decision, not a dashboard export. The audit should tell the store which parts of the current path are clear, which parts need a closer look, and who owns the next check. If the team cannot name the shopper, the offer, and the next step, more reporting will not fix the gap.</p><p>Limit the review to one store and one recent period the team can still remember. A month of campaigns is usually enough to see patterns without mixing old offers into current inventory. Write down three things before you open an ad account:</p><ul><li>The vehicles, events, or offers the store meant to feature.</li><li>The action a shopper was asked to take.</li><li>The person or team who was supposed to continue the conversation.</li></ul><p>Those three notes keep the audit tied to the store. They also stop the review from turning into a generic channel lecture. A franchise store and an established independent store can use the same questions. The answers will differ because inventory, offers, and staffing differ.</p><p>Keep the audit separate from work that already has its own home. A <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/franchise-dealership-marketing-plan">franchise dealership marketing plan</a> decides what to coordinate next. A calendar decides when work happens. This review checks whether the current path is still intact.</p><h2>Who should sit in the review</h2><p>One person can collect screenshots. The judgment needs the people who touch the shopper. Invite the marketing lead, a sales or inventory manager, and someone from the business development or follow-up team. If an outside partner runs creative or media, ask them for the source files and the destination links. Do not ask them to grade their own work as the only finding.</p><p>Give each person one lane:</p><ul><li>Marketing confirms the message, the destination, and the campaign dates.</li><li>Sales or inventory confirms that the vehicles and offers are still real.</li><li>The follow-up team confirms where responses land and what the next step is.</li></ul><p>BDC Promotions describes its own work in the same split. Strategy starts with the store, inventory, market, and sales priorities. Creative covers static, event, inventory, and video advertising. Optimization refines paid campaigns around qualified opportunities. Nurture is the follow-up lane that keeps conversations moving. The appointment step is a scheduled visit with a day and time. An audit should be able to point to each of those handoffs, even when a different team owns them.</p><p>Agree on a written record. A shared note with the check, the evidence, and the owner is enough. Avoid a score that implies a guaranteed outcome. The homepage states the standard plainly: never imply guaranteed lead volume, cost per lead, sales, return on ad spend, or show rate.</p><h2>Check how shoppers discover the store</h2><p>Discoverability is the first section of the checklist. Look at the places a shopper can meet the current offer, then confirm that each place still points at a live destination. A strong ad cannot help if the page, listing, or message behind it is stale.</p><h3>Name the current destinations</h3><p>List the pages and listings the recent campaigns actually used. Include the store website, inventory or vehicle listing destinations, and any event or offer page. Open each one the way a shopper would, on a phone, without being logged into an ad account.</p><p>For each destination, answer four questions:</p><ul><li>Does the page load and show the vehicle, offer, or event named in the ad?</li><li>Can a shopper tell which store this is?</li><li>Is there one clear way to ask a question or request a next step?</li><li>Do the photos and details match what the store can still sell or host?</li></ul><p>If the answer to any question is no, mark that destination for a fix before you judge the media. Sending more people to a stale page makes the later numbers harder to trust.</p><h3>Match the message to the vehicle or event</h3><p>Creative should give shoppers a reason to respond. On the BDC Promotions site, that work includes static and new-car lead-gen ads, event ads, inventory creative, and video. The audit does not need to reshoot anything. It needs to see whether the live message still matches the brief.</p><p>Pull one recent example from each active format. <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/services/automotive-ad-creative">Automotive ad creative</a> on the BDC Promotions site is the reference for static, event, inventory, and video formats. Compare the words in the ad with the words on the destination. Note any offer that names a vehicle the store no longer has, an event date that has passed, or a call to action that lands on a generic page with no matching inventory. Those are audit findings, not creative opinions.</p><p>When the store wants a deeper look at message testing, use the separate <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/automotive-dealership-ad-creative-testing">ad creative testing framework</a>. This checklist only confirms that the current message and destination still agree.</p><h2>Review the path from click to conversation</h2><p>A conversion path is the route from a response to a useful next step. For this audit, "useful" means the shopper can ask a question, share contact details, or request a time. It does not mean a sale. Count the routes the store is actually using, then try each one.</p><h3>Walk every live response route</h3><p>Common routes are a site form, a phone number, a click-to-message button, and a lead form inside an ad platform. Submit or place a test only where the store allows it, and tell the follow-up team first so a test is not treated as a shopper.</p><p>Record what happens:</p><ul><li>Did the form accept the test and show a confirmation?</li><li>Did the phone number match the number published on the site?</li><li>Did the message thread open on the account the team watches?</li><li>Did the confirmation tell the shopper what happens next?</li></ul><p>BDC Promotions publishes a phone number, 352-207-1074, and a request form on its own site. A dealership audit should be just as specific. If two phone numbers or two forms are live, write down which campaigns use which one. Split routes hide missed responses.</p><h3>Look for a single next step</h3><p>Each campaign should ask for one primary action. Multiple buttons are fine when they support that action. They are a problem when one ad asks for a message, a call, an application, and a brochure at the same time. Pick the action the store can answer during staffed hours.</p><p>Read the confirmation with a shopper's eyes. It should say who will respond and what information is still needed. A thank-you page with no next step leaves the conversation to chance. The <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/process">process page</a> shows how BDC Promotions connects message, response, and a scheduled visit. Use it as a reference for the handoff, not as a claim that every store uses the same setup.</p><h2>Inspect paid media without relitigating the buy</h2><p>Paid media is the third section. The goal is to see whether current campaigns still point at approved offers and watched destinations. This is not a comparison of search ads and social ads, and it is not a budget rebuild.</p><p>Open each active campaign and record five facts:</p><ol><li>The campaign name and the dates it ran in the review window.</li><li>The offer or vehicle it was built to feature.</li><li>The destination URL or message thread.</li><li>The audience or location settings, in plain language.</li><li>The result the account is set to observe, such as a form, a call, or a message.</li></ol><p>Then check three links. The destination should still match the offer. The result the account observes should be the same action the follow-up team watches. The creative should have passed whatever approval the store requires before launch. BDC Promotions describes that approval as a review of the offer, details, format, and call to action before creative is used.</p><p>Flag any campaign whose destination is a dead page, whose offer has expired, or whose counted result never reaches the people who talk to shoppers. Those are audit items. Questions about why a specific ad set produced few leads belong in the separate guide on <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/why-are-my-dealership-ads-not-generating-leads">dealership ads that are not generating leads</a>.</p><p>Also separate brand standards from performance. Google's advertising policies require ads to be clear and honest, and they warn against omitting or obscuring information a person needs to make a decision. Read the current <a href="https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6008942?hl=en" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">Google Ads policies</a> when you review offer language. The audit should confirm that material conditions are visible. It should not invent a legal conclusion.</p><h2>Follow a lead until someone owns the next step</h2><p>Lead handling is where many audits stop too early. A response that nobody owns is not a finished path. Ask the follow-up team to trace one real response from each active route during the review window. Use a redacted example if customer details should stay private.</p><p>For each example, write down:</p><ul><li>Where the response arrived.</li><li>Who saw it first.</li><li>How the team replied, such as phone, message, or email.</li><li>Whether the shopper was offered a specific day and time.</li><li>Where the outcome was recorded.</li></ul><p>The point is the handoff, not a script. If the first reply has no owner, the audit has found a gap even when the ad account looks busy. BDC Promotions describes nurture as the follow-up lane that keeps a shopper conversation moving toward a scheduled visit. Stores that handle follow-up themselves should still be able to name the owner and the record.</p><p>Do not turn this section into a scoring model or a call script. Lead quality scoring and follow-up language have their own guides. Here, you only need to know whether a response can become an appointment the sales team can see.</p><p>Appointment confirmation deserves a quick check of its own. If the team sets visits, confirm how the shopper is reminded and how the store learns about a cancellation. The related <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/dealership-appointment-confirmation-process">appointment confirmation process</a> goes deeper. The audit just needs evidence that a set time does not disappear between the follow-up team and the showroom.</p><h2>Read the measurements the store can explain</h2><p>Measurement is the last section of the checklist. Review the numbers the team already uses. Then ask whether each number describes the path you just walked. A metric that nobody can define should not decide the next budget change.</p><p>Use a small table. Fill it with the store's own definitions, not with industry averages.</p><table><thead><tr><th>Check</th><th>What to confirm</th><th>A finding worth writing down</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Discoverability</td><td>The live page or listing matches the current vehicle, offer, or event.</td><td>The ad names a vehicle or date the destination no longer supports.</td></tr><tr><td>Conversion path</td><td>The form, call, or message reaches a person who is watching.</td><td>Two routes are live and only one is staffed.</td></tr><tr><td>Paid media</td><td>The campaign destination and observed result match the approved offer.</td><td>The account counts a result the follow-up team never sees.</td></tr><tr><td>Lead handling</td><td>A named owner can show the reply and the next step.</td><td>Responses arrive, but no one records who answered.</td></tr><tr><td>Measurement</td><td>The team can define each reported number in plain language.</td><td>A report uses a term that means different things to marketing and sales.</td></tr></tbody></table><p>Keep the math modest. If the store tracks spend, responses, conversations, and scheduled visits, note which of those the team can trace to a source. If a number cannot be traced, label it as incomplete instead of forcing a return-on-investment claim. Budget allocation and ROI calculators are separate projects. This audit only checks that the inputs exist and mean the same thing to everyone in the room.</p><p>The <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/dealership-lead-response-monitoring">lead response monitoring guide</a> covers ongoing metrics after the audit. Use that piece when the store is ready to watch the path week by week. Do not copy its metric list into this checklist.</p><h2>Turn the automotive dealership digital marketing audit into a short fix list</h2><p>End the meeting with a list the store can act on. Three to seven items is enough. Each item needs an owner, the evidence you saw, and a date to recheck. Rank them by broken handoffs first. A dead destination or an unwatched phone line matters more than a cosmetic creative note.</p><p>A practical close looks like this:</p><ul><li>Fix destinations that contradict the current offer.</li><li>Choose one watched route for each active campaign.</li><li>Name the person who owns the first reply.</li><li>Agree on the few numbers the next review will use.</li></ul><p>Share the note with the people who sat in the review. If an outside partner owes a destination change or a creative revision, the note should say so without blaming a person who was not there. Recheck only the items you wrote down. A fresh audit every week becomes a new project and hides whether the last fix worked.</p><p>Dealerships that want help walking the same path can look at the <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/services">automotive marketing services</a> and decide which piece belongs in-house. An audit checks the path already in market. It looks for broken links between the offer, the destination, the response route, and the follow-up. Build or refresh the plan after those breaks are visible.</p><h3>How often should a dealership run this checklist?</h3><p>Run it when offers, inventory, or staffing change enough to make old destinations risky. A scheduled pass once a quarter is a reasonable rhythm for many stores. Recheck sooner after a new campaign launch or a change in who answers responses. There is no universal interval.</p><h3>Does a low lead count mean the audit failed?</h3><p>No. Lead count is only one input, and this checklist does not treat it as a verdict. A campaign can follow the path and still produce fewer responses than the store hoped. The audit asks whether the path is intact and whether the team can explain the numbers it already has.</p><h3>Can one department complete the audit alone?</h3><p>One person can gather the links and screenshots. The findings are stronger when marketing, inventory or sales, and the follow-up team each confirm their own lane. A destination can look fine to marketing and still name a vehicle the store cannot deliver.</p><h3>What should the store avoid claiming after the review?</h3><p>Avoid any statement that a fix will guarantee leads, appointments, show rate, or sales. Also avoid publishing another store's results as if they were yours.
