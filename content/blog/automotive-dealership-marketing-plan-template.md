<p>What should an automotive dealership marketing plan include before the next campaign starts? A useful plan connects a business goal to a specific audience, message, channel, budget, lead-handling process, and measurement routine. Use this automotive dealership marketing plan template to make those decisions visible before anyone launches a campaign.</p><p data-mega-cta="primary"><a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/contact">Talk with BDC Promotions about your dealership's marketing plan</a></p><h2>What should the plan settle before campaign work begins?</h2><p>A marketing plan is not just a list of platforms or a calendar of posts. It is a shared set of decisions that explains what the dealership is trying to change and how the team will know whether its campaign is moving in the right direction. It should be specific enough that a manager, marketing partner, and sales or business development team can act on the same version.</p><p>Start with the business need. Is the dealership trying to create more conversations with shoppers, promote a particular sales opportunity, increase appointment activity, or make a message more consistent across channels? Keep the goal focused. If every campaign is expected to solve every sales and operations challenge, it will be difficult to choose a clear offer or evaluate results.</p><p>Then connect that need to a practical next step for the customer. A campaign might invite a shopper to ask about a vehicle, request information, start a Messenger conversation, or schedule a visit. The action should match what the dealership can handle. If staff cannot respond promptly to a particular type of inquiry, address that process before increasing the volume of inquiries.</p><p>BDC Promotions describes its work around social advertising, shopper engagement, and moving conversations toward appointments. Dealership leaders can review the available <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/services">automotive marketing services</a> while deciding which part of the customer journey a campaign should support. The plan should still identify the dealership's own goal and responsibilities, rather than assuming that choosing a channel alone will deliver the result.</p><h2>How do you set a campaign goal you can evaluate?</h2><p>Write the goal in one sentence. Include the business outcome, the audience or opportunity, and the period the team will review. For example: “During the campaign period, generate shopper inquiries for the selected offer and review how many become two-way conversations and appointments.” This is more useful than “get more engagement” because it gives the team a reason to track what happens after someone responds.</p><p>Separate outcomes from activity measures. Impressions, clicks, and video views can help explain whether people encountered or interacted with an ad. They do not, by themselves, show that a shopper was reached by the dealership or that an appointment was made. If the objective is appointment opportunity, include the lead stages that connect advertising activity to that outcome.</p><ul><li><strong>Business outcome:</strong> the result the dealership wants to support, such as more shopper conversations or appointment opportunities.</li><li><strong>Campaign measures:</strong> delivery and response indicators, such as reach, clicks, form submissions, or messages.</li><li><strong>Follow-through measures:</strong> the next steps the store can verify, such as attempted contact, two-way conversation, appointment set, or appointment shown.</li><li><strong>Review period:</strong> when the team will check progress and decide whether to keep, adjust, or stop the campaign.</li></ul><p>Record a starting point before launch when the dealership has reliable historical information. If tracking is incomplete or the campaign is new, say so. Do not invent a baseline or promise a result. Instead, define how the team will capture comparable information during the campaign so later decisions rest on more than impressions or opinions.</p><h2>Who is the campaign for, and what do they need to know?</h2><p>Define the intended audience in practical terms. A plan can name a vehicle category, an offer, a geographic market, or a stage in the shopping process, depending on the campaign. Avoid a broad description such as “everyone who might buy a car.” A more focused audience helps the team judge whether the message, creative, and response path fit together.</p><p>Next, write down what the shopper needs to understand quickly. What is being offered or discussed? Why might it matter to this person? What should they do next? Keep the message direct and make sure the underlying offer is accurate, available, and approved for public use. If the campaign depends on details such as eligibility, timing, vehicle availability, or terms, confirm them with the responsible dealership staff before creative is produced.</p><p>Use a simple message brief:</p><ul><li><strong>Audience:</strong> Who should notice this campaign?</li><li><strong>Need or question:</strong> What might that shopper be trying to resolve?</li><li><strong>Main point:</strong> What one thing should the shopper remember?</li><li><strong>Proof or detail:</strong> What accurate information makes the message useful?</li><li><strong>Action:</strong> What should the shopper do after seeing it?</li></ul><p>For a campaign aimed at creating vehicle inquiries, for instance, do not leave the audience guessing which inventory or offer is relevant. Identify the intended vehicle group and the information the store can confirm. For a broader awareness campaign, clarify what follow-up action will count as a meaningful response. A clear brief prevents the ad, landing experience, and employee response from telling different stories.</p><h2>Which channels belong in the plan?</h2><p>Choose channels based on the goal, audience, available material, and the dealership's ability to handle the response. A channel should have a defined job. For example, paid social can introduce a message and invite a shopper to respond; a website page can provide details; Messenger or a form can capture an inquiry; and the store's staff can continue the conversation. The plan should explain these handoffs instead of treating channels as separate tasks.</p><p>Do not add channels just to make the plan appear comprehensive. Every additional channel requires appropriate creative, ownership, monitoring, and a way to assess what it contributes. If the team has limited time or a modest campaign budget, it may be more useful to make one or two connected paths work well than to spread attention across many disconnected activities.</p><p>Use this comparison to decide what each option needs before it is added:</p><table><thead><tr><th>Plan element</th><th>Paid social campaign</th><th>Dealership website or landing page</th><th>Direct shopper response path</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Primary role</td><td>Put a focused message in front of an intended audience and invite action.</td><td>Give shoppers a place to review relevant details and take the next step.</td><td>Continue the conversation after a shopper responds.</td></tr><tr><td>Ready when</td><td>Audience, approved creative, offer, budget, and action are defined.</td><td>Information is accurate, the page works on mobile, and contact options are clear.</td><td>An owner, response method, and handoff process are agreed.</td></tr><tr><td>What to review</td><td>Delivery, response, and whether the intended audience is engaging.</td><td>Page visits, completed actions, and any usability or tracking issues.</td><td>Response attempts, two-way conversations, and progression to the intended next step.</td></tr><tr><td>Common planning gap</td><td>Creative attracts attention but does not say what to do.</td><td>Ad promise and page content do not match, or the contact route is unclear.</td><td>Responses arrive without a clear person or process responsible for follow-up.</td></tr></tbody></table><p>When considering channels or service support, distinguish what the dealership needs from what a provider actually offers. A marketing plan should name the scope being considered, the intended handoff, and the dealership's role in responding.</p><h2>How should the budget and timing be recorded?</h2><p>For each campaign, separate advertising spend from production, management, or other costs if they apply. State the amount, who approves it, the dates it covers, and whether it is a fixed test or part of ongoing activity. Do not assume that a service fee and a media budget are the same line item. Confirm the applicable agreement and the platform account responsible for spend before launch.</p><p>Make the timing concrete. List the date the offer and details must be confirmed, the date creative is due for review, the approval deadline, the planned start and end dates, and the dates for performance checks. Build in enough time for changes. If the campaign is tied to a sale event or other deadline, clarify what happens if approvals or assets are late. A schedule with dependencies is more useful than a single launch date.</p><p>Budget and time should also reflect the follow-up workload. A campaign that generates responses needs staff capacity to monitor those responses and continue conversations. Decide who covers each channel, including nights, weekends, or time away, if those periods are relevant to the campaign. If capacity is limited, set expectations and adjust the campaign plan before launch rather than discovering the gap after responses arrive.</p><h2>What creative and approvals should be ready?</h2><p>List the assets the campaign needs: image or video, copy, headline, call to action, destination page, and any offer details or disclaimers. Specify the intended audience and placement for each asset. A video should have an opening that makes its subject clear, and the visual material should relate to the offer rather than functioning as unrelated decoration. The message must remain understandable even when a viewer sees only a short portion of it.</p><p>Assign an owner to confirm facts, branding, and availability. Then identify who can approve the final version and where approval will be recorded. “Looks good” in an informal conversation may leave uncertainty about which version was approved. A written approval attached to the exact final asset helps avoid an accidental launch of an earlier draft.</p><ul><li>Confirm that the offer, vehicles, dates, and terms are accurate.</li><li>Check that the dealership's brand and contact details are correct.</li><li>Review the opening, on-screen text, caption, and call to action together.</li><li>Test that the destination matches the ad and works on a phone.</li><li>Record approval for each final asset and note any required revision.</li></ul><p>Keep a version label and final approval date in the plan. If an asset changes after approval, send the revised version back through review. That small control gives dealership leaders visibility into what is live and helps the marketing and sales teams work from the same campaign message.</p><h2>How will a shopper's response be handled?</h2><p>Before launch, map the route from the ad to a real response and then to the next step. Identify whether a shopper will use a form, call, or message. For each path, say who sees the inquiry, who responds, what information is recorded, and how the team will know whether contact was made. A campaign is not complete when a form is submitted; the plan needs to account for the follow-up that gives the shopper a useful next step.</p><p>Set a response expectation the dealership can meet and decide how it will be monitored. The exact timing standard should reflect the store's staffing and process. Establish a backup owner for unassigned inquiries and a way to surface items that have not received attention. If the campaign uses a social messaging path, decide who checks the inbox and how conversations are routed to the appropriate team member.</p><p>Map a few common outcomes in advance: the shopper is reached, the shopper asks for more information, an appointment is discussed, or the shopper does not respond. Give staff a clear next action for each case. Existing <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/dealership-lead-follow-up-scripts">dealership lead follow-up examples</a> can be a useful reference when the team is agreeing on consistent, respectful language. Adapt any script to the store's policies and the conversation rather than sending a message that feels automatic or irrelevant.</p><p>Also decide how the team will handle a mismatch. If an advertised vehicle is no longer available, or a detail changes, specify who updates the campaign and who informs shoppers already in conversation. Clear ownership reduces the chance that advertising continues to invite a response to an offer the store can no longer support.</p><h2>What should the team measure after launch?</h2><p>Choose a small set of measures before the campaign starts. Use both campaign-level indicators and follow-through measures. Depending on the goal, the team might review spend, delivery, clicks, forms or messages, attempted responses, two-way conversations, and appointment progression. A measure is useful only if it is defined consistently and someone is responsible for recording it.</p><p>Write down what counts. Does a lead mean a completed form, a phone call, or any conversation? Does an appointment count when it is offered, scheduled, or confirmed? If the team uses different definitions, a dashboard may look precise while masking disagreement about what happened. Document the definitions and the system or report that supplies each figure.</p><p>Review results in context. A low number of inquiries could reflect delivery, the offer, the audience, or a response path that is difficult to use. A high number of inquiries does not automatically mean the campaign is producing useful conversations. Compare each stage and discuss where the journey is losing momentum before changing the creative or budget. Where there is too little information to draw a conclusion, record that limitation and decide what additional observation is needed.</p><p>Set a regular review cadence that gives the campaign enough time and data to evaluate, while still allowing the team to catch broken links, inaccurate details, or unhandled responses quickly. Separate operational checks from strategic decisions: check that the campaign is running as approved and inquiries are routed; then review whether the plan should change. Keep a simple change log with the date, reason, and expected effect of each adjustment.</p><p>For broader industry context, dealership leaders can consult the <a href="https://www.nada.org/" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">National Automobile Dealers Association</a> as they develop their internal priorities. Use your own dealership's records and agreed definitions when judging a campaign; a general industry resource is not a substitute for checking what happened in your store.</p><h2>What can you copy into an automotive dealership marketing plan template?</h2><p>Use the outline below as a working document. Keep it on one shared page or in a system the people responsible can access. Mark unknowns explicitly and assign an owner and due date to resolve them. The goal is not to complete every field for its own sake; it is to expose decisions or dependencies that could disrupt the campaign.</p><ol><li><strong>Campaign name and dates:</strong> Name the effort, planned start and end, and review dates.</li><li><strong>Business goal:</strong> State the outcome the dealership wants to support and the reason it matters now.</li><li><strong>Audience and opportunity:</strong> Describe the intended shopper, market, offer, or vehicle group.</li><li><strong>Message and action:</strong> Write the main point, supporting details, and the one next step to request.</li><li><strong>Channels and destinations:</strong> Give each channel a job and list the page, phone, form, or messaging path.</li><li><strong>Budget and approvals:</strong> Record media allocation, other agreed costs, approver, and approved scope.</li><li><strong>Creative and asset owners:</strong> List each asset, its owner, review status, and final approved version.</li><li><strong>Response process:</strong> Identify the primary and backup owners, expected handling, and escalation path.</li><li><strong>Measures and definitions:</strong> Name the reporting source, what each measure means, and who updates it.</li><li><strong>Risks and dependencies:</strong> Note offer changes, inventory checks, staffing gaps, tracking needs, or delayed approvals.</li><li><strong>Decision log:</strong> Record changes, their dates, the reason, and who authorized them.</li></ol><p>For example, a team might mark an offer confirmation as “pending” and assign it to a manager with a due date. That is better than filling the plan with a guessed detail. The same applies to tracking: if the team has not verified that a form or call is being counted correctly, label the measure as unverified and make testing a pre-launch task.</p><p>Dealerships that want to compare campaign support options can review the <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/services/automotive-ad-creative">automotive ad creative service page</a> alongside the broader services overview. Ask any prospective partner to clarify scope, responsibilities, review steps, reporting, and the dealership's approval role. The plan should make those answers explicit before work begins.</p><h2>What should you check on the final pre-launch review?</h2><p>Run a short go/no-go check with the people who own the campaign and the people who will handle the response. Do not treat the scheduled date as approval to launch if a key detail remains unresolved. A small review can catch a wrong destination, incomplete staffing plan, or asset that has not received final sign-off.</p><ul><li>Is the goal specific, and does the chosen call to action fit it?</li><li>Are audience, offer, timing, and public-facing details confirmed?</li><li>Are final creative and destination pages approved and consistent?</li><li>Have forms, phone links, messaging routes, and tracking been tested?</li><li>Does someone own every response path, with a backup?</li><li>Can the team see and define the measures it plans to review?</li><li>Are the budget, launch dates, responsibilities, and change process agreed?</li></ul><p>If an answer is no, decide whether to resolve the issue, narrow the campaign, or move the launch. Write down the decision and notify the people affected. A clear delay with an owner and next date is preferable to launching with a preventable gap and leaving staff to improvise.</p><p data-mega-cta="secondary"><a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/contact">Request a conversation about campaign planning</a></p><p>A dependable dealership marketing plan brings the goal, shopper message, budget, creative, response process, and measurement into one shared view.
