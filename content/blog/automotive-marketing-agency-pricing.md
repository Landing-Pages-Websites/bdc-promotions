<p>Automotive marketing agency pricing can be hard to compare when proposals use different fee models or bundle different work. The right comparison starts with scope: what the agency will do, what the dealership must provide, and what is paid to advertising platforms. Once those parts are separated, dealership leaders can judge whether proposals address the same need instead of choosing by headline fee alone.</p><p data-mega-cta="primary"><a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/contact">Discuss your dealership’s marketing needs</a></p><h2>What should a dealership include in the total cost?</h2><p>A proposal total may combine several distinct costs. Before comparing amounts, identify each one and who receives the payment. A quote that includes campaign management but excludes media spend is not directly comparable to a quote that combines both. Nor does a large scope automatically mean better value if it includes work the store does not need.</p><ul><li><strong>Agency fee:</strong> The charge for the agreed service, such as campaign planning, setup, management, creative coordination, reporting, or lead engagement. The written scope should state which tasks are included.</li><li><strong>Media budget:</strong> Money used to run advertisements on a platform. Ask whether this is paid by the dealership directly or handled through another billing arrangement, and who has authority to change the budget.</li><li><strong>Creative production:</strong> Clarify whether the work includes concepts, copy, graphics, video editing or production, and how many deliverables and review rounds are covered.</li><li><strong>Technology and access:</strong> Identify any separate tools, tracking setup, landing pages, or account access requirements. Confirm who owns accounts and assets and who maintains them.</li><li><strong>Internal labor:</strong> Staff time is part of the dealership’s practical investment, even if it does not appear on the agency invoice. Someone may need to approve creative, provide inventory or offer details, and follow up with inquiries.</li></ul><p>Write down whether every cost is recurring, one-time, variable, or conditional. For example, a setup fee may occur once, while a monthly management fee repeats. A media budget can change independently of the service fee. If an item is excluded, record who will complete it and what happens if it is delayed.</p><p>This distinction is especially important when leads are part of the desired outcome. A campaign can create an opportunity to speak with a shopper, but inquiry response and appointment follow-through require clear ownership. Its <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/services">automotive marketing services</a> page is one place to understand the service categories to discuss.</p><h2>Which agency pricing models might appear in a proposal?</h2><p>There is no single fee structure used by every provider. Common models include a fixed fee, a percentage of media spend, a project fee, or a blended arrangement. The label alone does not tell you whether the agreement is a good fit. Focus on how the fee is calculated, what the provider delivers, and whether the arrangement supports a clear decision about budget and performance.</p><table><thead><tr><th>Model</th><th>How it is structured</th><th>Questions to resolve</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Fixed management fee</td><td>A set recurring charge for an agreed service scope.</td><td>Which channels, tasks, deliverables, meetings, and support are included? What is excluded?</td></tr><tr><td>Percentage of media spend</td><td>The management charge is calculated in relation to advertising budget.</td><td>What spend is counted? Is there a minimum or a cap? How does the fee change if budget changes?</td></tr><tr><td>Project fee</td><td>A one-time charge for a defined setup or specific deliverable.</td><td>What will be delivered, by when, with how many revisions? Who maintains it afterward?</td></tr><tr><td>Blended or custom fee</td><td>Several services or fee types are combined.</td><td>Can each component be itemized? Which charges recur, and what triggers a scope change?</td></tr></tbody></table><p>A fixed fee can make budgeting easier when the work stays stable, but it does not guarantee that every task is included. A spend-based fee may move with the media budget, so the dealership should understand the calculation before increasing investment. A project fee can suit a bounded task, though ongoing management may be separate. A blended proposal can be appropriate when components are clear; it is harder to assess when the line items are hidden inside one total.</p><p>Ask for a sample month or delivery cycle. The provider should be able to describe what happens, who does it, and how often. For instance, “campaign management” might mean periodic adjustments and reporting, or it might also include creative updates and inquiry engagement. Do not treat those as equivalent unless the scope says so.</p><h2>What factors change the amount of work?</h2><p>Agency pricing reflects the work required, not just the number of channels in a plan. A dealership can use the following factors to make its brief more precise and to understand why proposals differ.</p><ul><li><strong>Number of locations:</strong> Multiple rooftops may have different audiences, inventory priorities, approval owners, and reporting needs. Ask whether the proposal treats them separately or as one coordinated program.</li><li><strong>Channel mix:</strong> Each channel has its own setup, creative formats, account access, and measurement considerations. Adding a channel should come with a defined role, not just an additional line on the plan.</li><li><strong>Campaign variety:</strong> A single ongoing message may require less coordination than several campaigns for different vehicle categories, audiences, or promotional periods.</li><li><strong>Creative frequency:</strong> Agree on how often concepts are refreshed and what counts as a new asset. Reformatting existing approved material is different from developing a new concept or producing new footage.</li><li><strong>Approval complexity:</strong> Multiple reviewers and long approval cycles affect launch timing. Name the person who consolidates feedback and the expected review window.</li><li><strong>Lead-response responsibilities:</strong> Specify whether the work ends at lead generation or includes a defined communication and appointment-related role. State how leads are routed and who owns the next action.</li><li><strong>Measurement readiness:</strong> Account permissions, forms, CRM processes, and consistent definitions affect how results can be reviewed. Confirm what can be measured and what cannot before setting expectations.</li><li><strong>Communication needs:</strong> Establish meeting cadence, report format, response expectations, and the route for urgent campaign changes.</li></ul><p>These factors are not a formula for predicting a universal rate. They help explain where staff time and coordination may go. A store with one location, one priority, and a clear approval path may need a different scope from a group coordinating several locations and several sets of stakeholders.</p><h2>How can you compare two proposals fairly?</h2><p>Give each provider the same short brief and ask for a response in the same format. Without a shared brief, different proposals may solve different problems. A comparable request should name the dealership’s priority, the target customer action, the current campaign situation, the available internal support, and any constraints on budget, approvals, or timing.</p><ol><li><strong>State the business priority.</strong> Decide whether the immediate need is more shopper conversations, qualified inquiries, appointment opportunities, or a clearer way to assess current campaigns. Avoid asking a provider to solve everything at once.</li><li><strong>Describe the current process.</strong> Note what channels are active, who responds to inquiries, how appointments are handled, and where information is recorded. Do not assume a vendor can infer the workflow.</li><li><strong>Request an itemized scope.</strong> Ask for tasks, deliverables, frequency, assigned owner, and exclusions. Include setup, ongoing management, creative work, reporting, and any lead engagement that is proposed.</li><li><strong>Separate agency charges from media.</strong> Request the fee and platform budget as separate figures, with a written explanation of billing and account control.</li><li><strong>Agree on definitions.</strong> Specify what the report calls an inquiry, a qualified lead, a reached shopper, and an appointment. Keep activity measures distinct from downstream sales outcomes.</li><li><strong>Review the agreement.</strong> Check term, renewal, cancellation notice, payment timing, account access, data handling, and ownership or transfer of creative and campaign assets.</li><li><strong>Set change rules.</strong> Ask how additional locations, new channels, higher volume, or extra creative requests affect the fee. Make the process for approving a change explicit.</li></ol><p>A simple comparison sheet can keep the decision grounded. Use one row for each proposal and columns for scope, agency fee, media budget, one-time costs, dealership duties, reporting, contract terms, and open questions. If one cell is blank, treat it as a question to resolve, not as an assumption that the work is included.</p><p>Dealership leaders can also review BDC Promotions’ <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/process">campaign process</a> to see how its stated steps move from learning about a store to building a campaign, engaging shoppers, and working toward appointments. The purpose of reviewing a process is to prompt concrete scope questions, not to assume every proposal includes identical work.</p><h2>What does a useful proposal comparison look like?</h2><p>Consider a hypothetical dealership reviewing two proposals. Proposal A has a lower management fee but includes campaign management only; the dealership supplies creative, approves changes, and handles every inquiry. Proposal B costs more but specifies creative deliverables, reporting, and a defined lead-engagement responsibility. Comparing the totals alone would obscure the difference. The dealership must decide whether those additional responsibilities address a real operational need.</p><p>To make the comparison, the store can build a side-by-side worksheet:</p><ul><li><strong>List what each provider will do:</strong> Identify setup, campaign adjustments, creative, reporting, meetings, and any lead-related tasks. Avoid broad labels without a task description.</li><li><strong>List what the store will do:</strong> Include providing offer details, inventory priorities, access, approvals, and handling or routing shopper responses.</li><li><strong>Estimate internal effort:</strong> Ask each department owner how much time approvals and handoffs are likely to take. Use a reasonable planning estimate rather than pretending staff time is free.</li><li><strong>Record all cash costs:</strong> Separate management, media, project charges, and optional items. Mark which costs may vary and why.</li><li><strong>Check the outcome path:</strong> Trace how an ad response becomes a conversation, how the next action is assigned, and how the dealership will know whether the process progressed.</li></ul><p>Now test a change. Suppose the store adds another location or asks for more creative versions. Which proposal explains how that affects work and charges? If neither does, ask both providers to price the same change before deciding. The goal is not to force the providers into an identical model; it is to reveal the practical consequences of each arrangement.</p><p>This exercise also helps identify a mismatch. A dealership that lacks time to handle inquiries may not be served by a plan that ends at lead generation. A store with internal creative staff may not want to pay for production it does not need. Ask whether providers can scope distinct offerings so the dealership understands what it can select, rather than assuming every component is mandatory.</p><h2>How should a dealership assess media spend and results?</h2><p>Media spend is the amount used to distribute advertising; it is not the same as the fee for planning or managing a campaign. Ask where the budget is paid, who controls it, whether it can be changed without a new agreement, and how the provider will notify the dealership about pacing. The dealership should be able to see the planned budget and distinguish it from service charges.</p><p>Do not select a budget only because it appears in another store’s proposal or a generic benchmark. Consider the objective, market, campaign duration, audience, available inventory, creative, and the dealership’s capacity to respond. The right review question is whether the campaign has enough time and operating support to produce useful evidence for the next decision, not whether a number sounds impressive.</p><p>Before launch, agree on what the report will show. It may include delivery and engagement measures, inquiries, response progress, and appointment outcomes where the store can reliably record them. Define the source for each measure. A click or form submission is not the same as an attended appointment or a vehicle sale. If a conversion cannot be attributed or tracked, say so rather than presenting it as a known result.</p><p>For a practical measurement discussion, review BDC Promotions’ guide to <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/paid-social-advertising-roi-automotive-dealerships">paid social advertising ROI for dealerships</a> and its <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/dealership-marketing-roi-calculator">dealership marketing ROI calculation approach</a>. Use those resources to frame what a proposal should measure, while confirming which data your own dealership can access and explain.</p><h2>What should the agreement clarify about vehicle advertising?</h2><p>Marketing service fees are one issue; the content of vehicle advertisements is another. When campaigns mention vehicle prices, discounts, or conditions, the dealership should confirm that the wording and disclosures are accurate and approved. The Federal Trade Commission’s <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/automobile-industry-pricing-transparency-faqs" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">Automobile Industry Pricing Transparency FAQs</a> address representations about advertised vehicle prices and qualifying discounts. That source concerns vehicle advertising, not how marketing agencies set their fees, so it should not be used as a benchmark for agency pricing.</p><p>Establish who supplies and verifies price and offer details before creative is approved. The contract or workflow should identify the dealership reviewer responsible for confirming current terms, eligibility conditions, and any required disclosures. This prevents a pricing proposal from being confused with approval of the underlying vehicle-ad content.</p><h2>What questions should you ask before signing?</h2><p>Use these questions in a consultation and request written answers for anything that affects cost, ownership, or the expected workload:</p><ul><li>What work is included in the recurring fee, and what would be billed separately?</li><li>Is media spend separate from the agency fee, and who controls the advertising account?</li><li>What campaign, creative, and reporting deliverables are included, and how often are they provided?</li><li>How many review rounds are included, and who approves materials before launch?</li><li>Who responds to inquiries, by which channels, and what is the handoff to the dealership team?</li><li>How are leads, qualified opportunities, and appointments defined in reporting?</li><li>What permissions, dealership data, or materials are needed to begin?</li><li>What are the agreement term, renewal, cancellation, and asset-transfer conditions?</li><li>What changes would alter the scope or fee, and how will the dealership approve those changes?</li><li>What results can be observed with the current tracking setup, and what limitations should the report disclose?</li></ul><p>Answers should be specific enough for an owner, general manager, or marketing lead to understand the commitment. If the proposal uses broad wording such as “full service,” ask for the tasks behind it. If an expected outcome depends on the dealership’s response process, name that dependency. A clear review routine helps the dealership see whether the agreed work still matches its needs and whether a change request is justified. Set a recurring meeting or review point in the agreement, and use the same agenda each time. This prevents a conversation about results from turning into an unstructured discussion about adding tasks.</p><ol><li><strong>Confirm delivery:</strong> Compare completed work with the proposal. Note which campaign tasks, creative assets, and reports were delivered, and which items are pending.</li><li><strong>Review operating dependencies:</strong> Check whether the dealership supplied access, offer details, approvals, and lead follow-up as planned. A missed handoff can affect the campaign without changing the provider’s contracted task.</li><li><strong>Read the measures consistently:</strong> Use the definitions set before launch. If a metric or tracking method changed, document when and why, rather than comparing unlike periods as if they were identical.</li><li><strong>Identify a specific adjustment:</strong> State the issue, proposed action, owner, and expected review date. Examples include clarifying a lead-routing step or changing the frequency of an agreed creative review.</li><li><strong>Approve scope changes in writing:</strong> If the proposed adjustment adds work or cost, request a revised scope and confirm it before the work begins.</li></ol><p>Keep a short decision log with the date, the question reviewed, the evidence available, the decision, and the person responsible for the next step. This record is useful when the dealership reviews renewal or compares a revised proposal with the original. It also separates a genuine service-scope change from a temporary performance concern that may need more observation or better measurement.</p><p>A renewal review should revisit the original business priority, actual deliverables, internal workload, media budget, and tracking limitations. Ask what should stay the same, what should change, and what evidence supports that choice. If the dealership cannot explain the work being purchased or how the next decision will be made, pause to clarify the scope before renewing or expanding it.</p><h2>Frequently asked questions</h2><h3>Is an agency fee the same as advertising spend?</h3><p>No. The agency fee pays for the services stated in the agreement. Advertising spend is used to distribute ads on a platform. Ask for separate amounts and clear billing responsibility for each.</p><h3>Should a dealership choose the lowest proposal?</h3><p>Not on price alone. Compare deliverables, exclusions, staff responsibilities, measurement, account access, and contract terms. A lower fee can leave tasks to the store that another provider has included.</p><h3>Can an agency promise a certain number of sales?</h3><p>Do not assume that a sales or appointment commitment exists. Ask what is being promised, what conditions apply, how the result is measured, and whether the commitment appears in the written agreement. Distinguish a forecast from a contractual commitment.</p><h3>What should a dealership bring to a pricing discussion?</h3><p>Bring the dealership’s priorities, current channel mix, lead-handling process, available internal support, approval workflow, and questions about account control and reporting. A clear brief helps the provider explain what work is relevant and what information is still needed.</p><p>Automotive marketing agency pricing becomes easier to evaluate when fees, media, deliverables, and responsibilities are separated. Compare proposals against the same dealership priorities, resolve open questions in writing, and choose a scope your team can support and measure.</p>
