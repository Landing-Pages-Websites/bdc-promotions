<p>A useful car dealer lead source comparison asks what happened after a lead arrived, not just how many records each channel produced. Compare cost per qualified lead, appointment set rate, show rate, sold rate, response time, and attribution on the same footing. That gives owners, marketing managers, and BDC leaders a better basis for deciding what to keep, change, or test.</p><p data-mega-cta="primary"><a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/contact">Discuss your dealership's lead goals</a></p><h2>Why is lead volume an incomplete comparison?</h2><p>Lead count is easy to see, but it does not say whether the dealership received contactable shoppers, whether the team reached them, or whether they arrived for an appointment. A channel can deliver many form submissions and still create little usable opportunity. Another may generate fewer inquiries but a larger share of conversations that progress.</p><p>Volume also depends on how a lead is defined. One report may count every form submission. Another may exclude duplicates, invalid contact details, or people outside the dealership's sales area. If those definitions differ, a simple cost-per-lead comparison can reward the source that counts more generously rather than the source that helps the store.</p><p>Use lead volume as the first measure in a chain, not the final verdict. A practical comparison follows the shopper through a consistent set of stages:</p><ul><li><strong>Lead received:</strong> A unique inquiry recorded with a source and timestamp.</li><li><strong>Qualified:</strong> The inquiry meets the dealership's agreed criteria for a viable sales opportunity.</li><li><strong>Contacted:</strong> The team made a documented attempt to reach the shopper, or established contact.</li><li><strong>Appointment set:</strong> The shopper agreed to a specific next step or visit.</li><li><strong>Appointment shown:</strong> The shopper arrived or completed the agreed appointment.</li><li><strong>Sold:</strong> The lead can be matched to a completed vehicle sale under a defined attribution rule.</li></ul><p>Not every store will use identical qualification rules, and that is fine. The essential point is to write down the rules and apply them consistently across sources. For context on the wider dealership industry, the <a href="https://www.nada.org/" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">National Automobile Dealers Association</a> is an industry resource; the comparison framework here is a management tool, not an industry benchmark.</p><h2>Which measures make a lead source comparison useful?</h2><p>Use a small set of measures that connect spend to the outcomes the dealership wants. Keep both efficiency and progression in view. The formulas below work with a channel, campaign, location, or time period, provided the same definitions and cohort rules are used throughout.</p><h3>Cost per qualified lead</h3><p><strong>Formula:</strong> source spend divided by the number of qualified leads from that source.</p><p>This is more useful than raw cost per lead when unqualified submissions vary between channels. Agree on what counts as qualified before evaluating results. Criteria might include valid contact information, a relevant vehicle-shopping inquiry, a dealership location the store serves, and no duplicate record within the chosen window. Use only criteria your team can apply reliably, and do not revise them midway through a comparison.</p><p>Separate the advertising cost from any management or production costs if those are available and meaningful to the decision. State what the figure includes. If one source has only media spend in the denominator and another includes additional costs, label the difference rather than presenting the figures as directly equivalent.</p><h3>Appointment set rate and show rate</h3><p><strong>Appointment set rate:</strong> appointments set divided by qualified leads. <strong>Show rate:</strong> appointments shown divided by appointments set. Use the same cohort: for example, leads received during a defined period that have had enough time to progress through the appointment window.</p><p>These measures help distinguish lead supply from follow-up and shopper intent. A low set rate may point to a mismatch between the inquiry and the offer, slow or inconsistent follow-up, or an appointment process that creates too much friction. A low show rate may call for examining confirmation, reminders, scheduling options, and how the team sets expectations. The rates alone do not prove the cause. Review the records and conversations behind the result before changing a campaign.</p><h3>Sold rate and cost per sale</h3><p><strong>Sold rate:</strong> attributable sales divided by qualified leads, or by appointments shown if that is the chosen measure. <strong>Cost per sale:</strong> the source cost divided by attributable sales. Name the denominator every time. A sold rate based on all leads is not directly comparable with one based only on shown appointments.</p><p>Sales attribution takes more care than counting a form. Shoppers may use multiple channels, return later, call instead of submitting a form, or buy from a different department. Select an attribution window and a matching rule, then apply them to every source. If the dealership cannot confidently match a sale to its originating inquiry, mark the result as uncertain instead of assigning credit by guesswork.</p><h3>Response time and contact rate</h3><p>Track elapsed time from receipt to the first meaningful attempt to respond, and define what qualifies as an attempt. Also track the share of leads the team actually reaches. Use the same business-hours convention for every channel. A lead received overnight should not be treated as if a staffed team had received it during the day unless that is how the dealership has chosen to report.</p><p>Speed and contactability are diagnostic measures, not stand-ins for sales. A source that supplies valid contact details may be easier to work, but that does not by itself establish profit or incremental sales. Check these measures alongside appointment and sales outcomes.</p><h2>How can a dealership compare sources on equal terms?</h2><p>Build one scorecard and use it for every source under review. The table is a template, not a set of industry targets. Fill it with the store's own results and mark unavailable or immature data honestly. Avoid treating a blank as zero.</p><table><thead><tr><th>Measure</th><th>Consistent definition</th><th>What it helps answer</th><th>Common caution</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Spend</td><td>Cost included for the source and period</td><td>What investment is being compared?</td><td>Do not mix media-only cost with a broader total without labeling it.</td></tr><tr><td>Qualified leads</td><td>Unique leads meeting documented store criteria</td><td>How much usable opportunity arrived?</td><td>Apply the same validity, duplicate, and service-area rules.</td></tr><tr><td>Cost per qualified lead</td><td>Spend divided by qualified leads</td><td>What does qualified opportunity cost?</td><td>Small or incomplete cohorts can make the figure unstable.</td></tr><tr><td>Appointment set rate</td><td>Appointments set divided by qualified leads</td><td>Are inquiries progressing to a scheduled next step?</td><td>Define what counts as an appointment and use a mature cohort.</td></tr><tr><td>Show rate</td><td>Appointments shown divided by appointments set</td><td>Are scheduled shoppers arriving?</td><td>Use appointments with enough time to occur.</td></tr><tr><td>Sold rate / cost per sale</td><td>Attributed sales divided by a named denominator; source cost divided by attributed sales</td><td>Does the source connect to a completed sale efficiently?</td><td>Record attribution rules and uncertainty.</td></tr><tr><td>Response and contact</td><td>Time to first attempt and share of leads reached</td><td>Can the team work the leads promptly?</td><td>Use one business-hours and attempt definition.</td></tr><tr><td>Attribution coverage</td><td>Share of records with usable source and outcome data</td><td>How much should the team trust the comparison?</td><td>Missing data lowers confidence; it does not mean no result.</td></tr></tbody></table><p>Read the measures together. A low cost per qualified lead is attractive only if leads progress and the attribution is credible. A high sold rate on a handful of records may be less dependable than a steadier pattern across a larger, more mature group. Conversely, a source should not be discarded simply because its sales have not had time to appear in the reporting system.</p><h2>How should the team define qualification and attribution?</h2><p>Write a short data dictionary before comparing channels. It should answer four questions:</p><ol><li><strong>What is a lead?</strong> Specify whether calls, messages, forms, and repeat submissions are included. Decide how duplicates are recognized and how they are counted.</li><li><strong>What makes it qualified?</strong> Set practical criteria that fit the dealership's sales process. Record the reason when an inquiry does not qualify, so a channel is not judged on an unexplained label.</li><li><strong>What is an appointment or a show?</strong> Define whether phone appointments count, how reschedules are handled, and what evidence confirms that the shopper arrived.</li><li><strong>When does a sale belong to a source?</strong> State the attribution window, how multiple touches are handled, and which CRM fields or records support the match.</li></ol><p>Keep the rules stable for the comparison period. When definitions need to change, record the date and avoid combining old and new periods as if they were identical. If a CRM field is incomplete or inconsistently populated, show the coverage rate. A clean process is more valuable than a detailed-looking report built on incompatible labels.</p><p>Attribution can be recorded at more than one level. A first-touch view asks where the shopper first became known. A last-touch view identifies the last recorded interaction before an outcome. A multi-touch view recognizes a sequence of interactions. Each answers a different question. Pick the view suited to the decision, show the rule, and do not describe an attributed sale as proof that a single channel caused it.</p><h2>How do you interpret the numbers without overreacting?</h2><p>Look for patterns across the journey and ask what the numbers do not show. Consider two hypothetical sources. Source A brings 100 inquiries, 40 qualified leads, 12 appointments, and 6 shows. Source B brings 60 inquiries, 36 qualified leads, 15 appointments, and 10 shows. If the investment and period were comparable, Source B would have fewer total inquiries but more qualified leads and shows in this example. The figures are invented only to illustrate the calculation; they are not a performance claim or expected result.</p><p>To see how the arithmetic works, suppose, purely for illustration, Source A spent $4,000 and Source B spent $3,000 in comparable periods. A's cost per qualified lead would be $100 ($4,000 divided by 40). B's would be about $83 ($3,000 divided by 36). A set 30% of qualified leads (12 divided by 40), while B set about 42% (15 divided by 36). The shown share of appointments would be 50% for A and about 67% for B. Those calculations describe only this invented example. They do not show whether either source generated sales or whether the underlying records are reliable.</p><p>That still would not settle the decision. The team would need to compare sales outcomes, response practices, and attribution coverage. If Source B's records were less complete, or its leads had a different mix, the apparent advantage could change. Use the funnel to generate questions, then inspect the underlying records.</p><p>When results appear weak, investigate the stage where progress slows:</p><ul><li><strong>Many inquiries, few qualified leads:</strong> Check targeting, creative promise, form friction, duplicate handling, and the qualification definition.</li><li><strong>Qualified leads but few contacts:</strong> Check contact details, routing, staff coverage, response time, and the logging of attempts.</li><li><strong>Contacts but few appointments:</strong> Review the conversation, availability, shopper needs, and whether the next step is clear.</li><li><strong>Appointments but few shows:</strong> Examine scheduling expectations, reminders, confirmation, and ease of rescheduling.</li><li><strong>Shows but few sales:</strong> Review the sales process and fit of the shopper's need, while avoiding the assumption that marketing alone explains the outcome.</li></ul><p>These are diagnostic prompts, not automatic conclusions. One weak stage can reflect several causes, and marketing, customer handling, inventory availability, and reporting may all matter. Talk with the people who handle the leads and review representative records before changing budget or creative.</p><h2>How can a dealership run a fair source comparison?</h2><p>A useful review can begin with existing CRM and campaign records. It does not require a complicated attribution model. It does require agreed definitions, consistent tracking, and a period long enough for the outcome being measured to occur.</p><p>Choose the observation window based on the decision and the time it takes shoppers to move through the store's process. A lead count can be reviewed soon after intake, while appointment shows and sales need more time. Set a date when the cohort will be considered mature, and distinguish records still in progress from completed outcomes. Comparing a recent source with a longer-running one can make the newer source appear weaker simply because its leads have had fewer chances to progress.</p><p>When practical, compare the same calendar period and similar operating conditions. Note weekends, holiday hours, staffing changes, inventory shifts, and campaign adjustments that may affect outcomes. If sources ran at different times or under different conditions, say so and treat the result as directional. Do not claim that a channel caused a difference just because its numbers changed alongside a campaign adjustment. A consistent time frame and a record of what changed make the comparison more useful, even when they cannot remove every outside influence.</p><ol><li><strong>Choose the decision.</strong> Are you deciding where to add budget, whether to continue a source, or what part of follow-up to improve? A focused question prevents a report from becoming a collection of unrelated metrics.</li><li><strong>Select comparable sources and dates.</strong> Note changes in spend, campaign setup, offers, staffing, operating hours, and major dealership conditions. If conditions differ, document them rather than hiding the difference.</li><li><strong>Confirm tracking before the start.</strong> Use a consistent source naming convention, capture timestamps, and make sure inquiries can be joined to dispositions and sales where appropriate. Include phone and messaging paths if the dealership can record them reliably.</li><li><strong>Set the qualification and attribution rules.</strong> Write the rules down and train the people entering outcomes. Leave time for appointments and sales to mature before finalizing the comparison.</li><li><strong>Calculate the same measures for each source.</strong> Report counts as well as rates. Counts show the volume behind a percentage; rates make different-sized sources easier to examine.</li><li><strong>Review quality and exceptions.</strong> Check duplicates, missing sources, unmatched sales, invalid contact details, and records that do not fit the usual path. Note how many records are affected.</li><li><strong>Make one measured change.</strong> If evidence points to an issue, adjust one major variable where practical, keep the tracking stable, and review the next comparable period. This makes it easier to understand what changed.</li></ol><p>Separate measurement from optimization. For example, a source could be generating viable inquiries while the dealership's follow-up process is inconsistent. Cutting the source immediately may conceal the process problem. Likewise, improving response time is not evidence that the source itself became more efficient. Record the change and compare the appropriate stage of the funnel.</p><h2>Which dealership lead sources should be compared?</h2><p>Compare the sources the store actually uses and can track with reasonable consistency. Depending on the dealership, that may include paid social, search advertising, third-party listings, organic website inquiries, phone calls, or repeat and referral opportunities. This is not a ranking of channels. The best source depends on the shopper, the dealership's goals, its ability to follow up, and the quality of its records.</p><p>Do not assume every channel should use the same creative, offer, or conversion path. Do insist that the measurement rules are comparable. Record the source and campaign clearly, preserve the initial inquiry details, and identify later touches where the system permits. If a shopper interacts with more than one source, flag that path rather than forcing a simple answer that the data cannot support.</p><p>Lead engagement and appointment generation are part of the journey after an inquiry arrives. Is the source missing at intake? Are leads not being assigned a disposition? Are appointments recorded in a calendar but not connected to the CRM record? Are sales entered without a source reference? Each gap calls for a different process fix.</p><p>For a first review, mark each outcome as known, unknown, or not yet mature. Show the number of records in each group. This prevents a team from silently treating unknown outcomes as failures, or from counting only the successful records that were easy to match. Add a confidence note to the scorecard: high, moderate, or low, with a brief reason such as source coverage or missing sales links.</p><p>Do not overstate precision. If an estimated cost per sale depends on incomplete attribution, label it as directional and explain the limitation. A reliable count of qualified opportunities may be more useful for the current decision than a precise-looking sales metric with weak matching. Improve the source capture and outcome logging, then revisit the sales comparison after more complete data accumulates.</p><h2>What questions should leaders ask in a monthly review?</h2><p>A short, repeatable review can keep decisions connected to evidence. Ask:</p><ul><li>Did each source use the same lead, qualification, appointment, and attribution definitions?</li><li>How many leads were unique, qualified, contacted, appointed, shown, and sold?</li><li>What were cost per qualified lead and the cost per outcome we trust?</li><li>Did response time or contact rate change, and was the change across all sources or one?</li><li>How many records are missing a source, disposition, appointment result, or sale match?</li><li>Are all cohorts mature enough for the outcome we are comparing?</li><li>What changed in budget, creative, staffing, inventory, hours, or process during this period?</li><li>What one next step would address the largest supported issue?</li></ul><p>Keep a brief decision log with the date, the evidence reviewed, the change made, and the measure that will determine whether to keep it. This prevents the team from repeating decisions without knowing what happened after the previous change. It also helps separate a short-term fluctuation from a durable pattern without pretending that any single period guarantees what comes next.</p><h2>How should a dealership decide what to do next?</h2><p>Use the comparison to make a bounded decision. Continue a source when it produces useful opportunities and the evidence supports its role. Investigate when one funnel stage is weak or the data is incomplete. Test a change when the reason for it is clear and the dealership can track the result. Reallocate budget only after checking costs, lead quality, sales maturity, and confidence in attribution together.</p><p>A sound car dealer lead source comparison is not a contest to find the channel with the most form fills. It is a consistent view of how investment becomes qualified conversations, appointments, shows, and sales opportunities, alongside the limits of the data. The numbers will not make every choice for you, but a shared scorecard can make the next choice more deliberate.</p><p>BDC Promotions works with automotive dealerships on paid social campaigns, lead engagement, and appointment generation.
