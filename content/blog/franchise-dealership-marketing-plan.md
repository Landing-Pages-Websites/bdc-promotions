<p>A strong franchise dealership marketing plan connects the vehicles a store needs to move with relevant offers, suitable channels, and a dependable response process. Instead of treating every model, promotion, and lead source as a separate campaign, build one operating plan that shows who each activity is for, what it should prompt them to do, and how the dealership will follow up. This guide walks through that structure, from setting priorities to reviewing results.</p><p data-mega-cta="primary"><a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/contact">Talk with the team about your dealership’s marketing</a></p><h2>What should a franchise dealership marketing plan coordinate?</h2><p>A useful plan is more than a media calendar. It is a shared set of choices that joins four parts of dealership activity:</p><ul><li><strong>Models:</strong> Which new or used vehicles, trims, or segments need attention?</li><li><strong>Offers:</strong> What is the shopper being invited to consider or do?</li><li><strong>Lead sources:</strong> Where will shoppers encounter the message and how will they respond?</li><li><strong>Follow-up:</strong> Who owns the response, what is the next step, and how will progress be recorded?</li></ul><p>If one part is missing, the campaign can create friction. An ad can draw interest in a vehicle that is no longer available. An offer may be too vague to prompt action. A lead may arrive through a channel the team is not watching. Or a response may fail to give the shopper a clear next step. The plan should make these handoffs visible before campaigns go live.</p><p>Start with business priorities, not a list of platforms. For each priority, write down the shopper, the vehicle or need, the message, the desired action, and the person or team responsible for the response. This gives the general manager, marketing lead, sales managers, and business development team a common working view. Dealerships looking at outside support can review the range of <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/services">automotive marketing services</a> as one part of deciding what work belongs in-house and what may need support.</p><h2>How should a dealership choose which models to feature?</h2><p>Model selection should reflect current store priorities and the shopper’s decision process. A franchise store may have several makes, body styles, price points, and model years competing for space in its marketing. Promoting everything equally can make the message unfocused and complicate sales follow-up.</p><p>Meet with the relevant sales and inventory leaders to agree on a short, current priority list. The goal is not to invent a complicated scoring system. It is to make the reason for featuring a vehicle clear. Consider these questions:</p><ul><li>Which vehicles or segments are the store actively trying to bring to shoppers’ attention?</li><li>Are the vehicles available and accurately represented on the site or listing destination?</li><li>Does the audience have a clear reason to consider this model now?</li><li>Can the sales team answer likely questions about availability, trim, price, and next steps?</li><li>When should the selection be reviewed because availability or priority may change?</li></ul><p>Group vehicles where it helps the shopper compare, such as by body style, use case, or broad price range. Keep a specific model message when a single vehicle or model line is the focus. Avoid a campaign that promises a particular vehicle experience while sending the shopper to a general page with no clear way to find it.</p><p>Give every featured group a named owner and a review date. Before publishing, confirm that the landing destination, imagery, details, and call to action still fit the current offer. When a priority changes, update the campaign and the response team’s reference materials together. This simple check reduces the chance that advertising and the showroom conversation tell different stories.</p><h2>How do you turn a model priority into a useful offer?</h2><p>An offer is the bridge between a vehicle and a shopper’s next step. It can emphasize a relevant benefit, a way to compare options, a scheduled visit, or a conversation with the dealership. It should be understandable without requiring a shopper to decode internal sales language.</p><p>For each offer, answer four questions in plain language:</p><ol><li><strong>Who is it for?</strong> Describe the shopper or situation, not just a broad demographic.</li><li><strong>What is the value?</strong> State the reason to pay attention. Make all material conditions clear in the ad or destination.</li><li><strong>What should the shopper do?</strong> Choose one primary action, such as exploring available models, asking a question, or arranging a visit.</li><li><strong>What happens next?</strong> Explain who will respond and what information the shopper should expect.</li></ol><p>Keep the offer and the destination aligned. If the message promotes a vehicle category, send the shopper to a page where that category is easy to explore. If it invites a conversation, make the contact route clear. Do not rely on a generic homepage as the only explanation of an offer.</p><p>Before an offer is used, have the appropriate dealership staff verify its availability, language, terms, and timing. This is especially important when offer details depend on eligibility or other conditions. Advertising should be accurate and clear; the Federal Trade Commission provides consumer and business resources at <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">FTC.gov</a>. Dealership teams can also consult the National Automobile Dealers Association for industry resources at <a href="https://www.nada.org/" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">NADA.org</a>. Use the dealership’s normal legal and compliance review for specific offer terms.</p><h2>How should channels and lead sources fit together?</h2><p>Choose channels according to the role they play, rather than assuming every campaign needs to appear everywhere. A channel plan should explain how a shopper may first see a message, where they can learn more, and how a response reaches the right team. It should also make clear which source will be used to review the resulting activity.</p><p>For each channel or lead source, record the following:</p><ul><li>The audience or shopping situation it is meant to reach.</li><li>The message, model priority, and offer it will carry.</li><li>The destination or response route, such as a vehicle page, lead form, phone call, or messaging conversation.</li><li>The team member or department responsible for monitoring and responding.</li><li>The source label or campaign information the dealership will use to distinguish it in reporting.</li></ul><p>Think about the complete path, not just the first click. A shopper may see a social post, visit a vehicle page later, and then call the store. Another may submit a form and expect a prompt response. A practical plan identifies each route the dealership intends to support and checks that staff know how to handle it.</p><p>For online campaigns, coordinate the ad’s promise with the page or conversation that follows. A message about a particular model should not lead to an unrelated destination. A message inviting contact should say what the shopper can ask about and how the dealership will respond. If the store is evaluating paid search, social, or other sources, compare them by audience, intent, operating needs, and the quality of conversations they help create—not just by the number of initial responses. It can sit alongside the campaign calendar, but it answers a different question: the calendar says when work happens; the plan says why the work exists and how its parts connect.</p><h2>How should the dealership plan for lead response?</h2><p>Marketing creates an opportunity only if the dealership can manage the response that follows. Decide who will see each type of inquiry, what information they need, and how they will move a conversation toward an appropriate next step. The process should cover web forms, phone calls, messaging, and any other sources the dealership actively uses.</p><p>Write a simple handoff for each source. Include the notification path, the person responsible, a backup owner, the first response goal set by the dealership, and where the result should be recorded. Specify what happens outside normal hours and how unresolved inquiries are surfaced to a manager. The exact process will depend on the store’s staffing and systems, so use a workflow the team can actually maintain.</p><p>Give staff a few flexible conversation prompts rather than a rigid script that ignores the shopper’s question. For example, a first response can acknowledge the vehicle or offer the shopper asked about, answer what is known, clarify any missing detail, and offer a relevant next step. Keep the conversation consistent with the campaign and avoid promising availability or terms that have not been verified.</p><p>Dealerships can use a written <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/dealership-lead-follow-up-scripts">lead follow-up script framework</a> to help teams prepare for common conversations. For a closer look at organizing the handoff, see the <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/process">BDC Promotions process overview</a>. The plan should still identify the dealership’s own owners and procedures; a vendor or channel does not replace internal accountability.</p><h2>Which measures help assess the plan?</h2><p>Use a short measurement set that connects activity to the outcome the dealership wants to improve. A single total lead count cannot show whether a particular model, offer, or source is producing relevant conversations. At the same time, avoid adding so many measures that nobody reviews them consistently.</p><p>A practical review can look at three levels:</p><ul><li><strong>Delivery:</strong> Did the planned campaign run, and did the intended audience have a chance to encounter it?</li><li><strong>Response:</strong> How many inquiries or conversations came through each route, and were they handled according to the store’s process?</li><li><strong>Progression:</strong> What share of responses advanced to a useful next step, such as a substantive conversation or appointment, based on the dealership’s own definitions?</li></ul><p>Compare like with like. Keep the time period, campaign purpose, source definitions, and lead-status rules consistent. Note operational changes that may affect comparisons, such as a change in staffing, inventory, offer, or response process. If lead quality is a concern, review examples and agree on what makes a lead useful before changing budgets or channels. The <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/dealership-lead-quality-scoring">dealership lead quality scoring framework</a> can help teams make those criteria more explicit.</p><p>Set a recurring review date. Ask what should continue, what needs adjustment, and what evidence supports that decision. If the answer is unclear, identify the missing information rather than making a confident change based on incomplete records. A useful plan is a learning tool: decisions become more specific as the dealership improves its source tracking and follow-up records.</p><h2>How can a dealership keep creative and execution aligned?</h2><p>Creative should make the model, shopper value, and action clear quickly. Review the opening impression, imagery, copy, offer details, destination, and response route together. A polished ad can still underperform its purpose if the opening is hard to understand or the next step is buried.</p><p>Before launch, use a brief quality check:</p><ol><li>Confirm the featured model or category matches the approved priority.</li><li>Check that visuals and copy are relevant to the vehicle and audience.</li><li>Make the opening message easy to understand and the action easy to find.</li><li>Verify that the destination works on a phone and reflects the same offer.</li><li>Test the lead route and confirm the right staff can receive and handle a response.</li><li>Have the responsible dealership decision-maker review the final version before it runs.</li></ol><p>Keep a record of what version ran, when it ran, and which plan entry it supported. When a campaign changes, record the reason and date. This makes later reviews more useful and helps the team distinguish a change in message from a change in audience, offer, or follow-up. A structured <a href="https://bdcpromotions.com/blog/automotive-dealership-ad-creative-testing">creative testing approach</a> can support more deliberate comparisons without changing several variables at once.</p><h2>What mistakes make a dealership plan hard to use?</h2><ul><li><strong>Promoting too many priorities at once:</strong> Reduce the active list and explain why each item deserves attention.</li><li><strong>Using an offer without a clear next action:</strong> Give the shopper one obvious step and make sure the destination supports it.</li><li><strong>Planning channels separately:</strong> Map how the message, destination, source label, and response owner connect.</li><li><strong>Measuring only volume:</strong> Review the quality and progression of conversations using consistent definitions.</li><li><strong>Leaving ownership implicit:</strong> Name the person or team responsible for every response route and review date.</li><li><strong>Letting plans go stale:</strong> Recheck model priorities, availability, offer details, links, and staffing on an agreed schedule.</li></ul><p>These issues are usually planning and coordination problems, not reasons to add more channels immediately. First make sure the current campaign has a clear purpose, a relevant message, a working response path, and an owner who can learn from the results.</p><h2>How can the plan become a repeatable operating routine?</h2><p>Use a short monthly or campaign-cycle meeting to align sales, marketing, and lead-response owners. Bring the current model priorities, offers, channel activity, response notes, and results. End the meeting with a small number of decisions, each assigned to a person and a date.</p><p>A straightforward cycle looks like this:</p><ol><li><strong>Set priorities:</strong> Confirm the vehicles or shopper needs the dealership wants to emphasize.</li><li><strong>Shape the offer:</strong> Agree on a clear, accurate message and the action it should prompt.</li><li><strong>Map the route:</strong> Choose channels and destinations, then name response owners.</li><li><strong>Check readiness:</strong> Verify creative, offer details, links, tracking, staffing, and approvals.</li><li><strong>Review evidence:</strong> Look at delivery, responses, and progression by source using consistent definitions.</li><li><strong>Decide what changes:</strong> Continue, adjust, pause, or test one specific element and document why.</li></ol><p>For a dealership assessing outside help, the plan also makes scope conversations more concrete. A campaign review date keeps updates intentional rather than leaving old assumptions in place.</p><h3>Should every model have its own campaign?</h3><p>Not necessarily. Group models when shoppers can usefully compare them under a shared need or message. Give a model a distinct campaign when the dealership has a clear reason, audience, offer, and destination for promoting it separately.</p><h3>Which lead source is best for a dealership?</h3><p>There is no universal best source for every store or objective. Compare sources using consistent definitions for cost, response handling, conversation quality, and progression, while accounting for the audience and purpose each source serves.</p><h3>What should a dealership do if lead volume rises but sales opportunities do not?</h3><p>Trace the path from message to response. Check whether the audience and offer are relevant, whether inquiries reach the correct team, and whether follow-up is timely and useful. Review examples and source records before deciding whether to change the campaign, the response process, or both.</p><p>A well-structured plan gives the dealership a clear connection between what it promotes, where shoppers respond, and how the team follows through. Keep the priorities focused, verify each handoff, and use consistent evidence to improve the next cycle.</p>
